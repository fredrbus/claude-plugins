# Orwell Writing

## Overview

Use Orwell's rules and ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English (STE) as practical filters for clear, direct, and honest prose. Use STE by default for technical, instructional, business, and product prose. Apply the rules to both drafting and revision, but do not erase deliberate voice, character, rhythm, humor, or genre when the user clearly wants them.

STE has writing rules and a controlled dictionary. Use an approved word with its approved meaning when the dictionary is available. Do not claim strict STE conformance without checking the current ASD-STE100 issue and dictionary.

## Language

Write in the language the user asks for. When the user does not say, write in the language of the request; when revising, keep the language of the source text. Do not translate unless the user asks for a translation.

Orwell's six rules are about honest, concrete thinking, so they carry over to any language. The STE writing rules carry over as principles. The STE dictionary does not: it is an English word list, so do not apply it to other languages or claim STE conformance for text that is not in English.

For Norwegian, follow the "Norwegian" section below.

## Core Rules

Remember these rules from "Politics and the English Language":

1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

Quote these rules as Orwell wrote them. When you write in another language, read rule 5 as "an everyday equivalent in the language you are writing in".

## ASD-STE100 baseline

For technical and instructional prose:

1. Use short sentences. Put one main action or statement in each sentence.
2. Use a clear subject and an active verb. Name the actor when the actor matters.
3. Use the same term for the same thing. Do not change a term only to avoid repetition.
4. Use familiar words with one precise meaning. Avoid idioms, slang, figurative language, and vague verbs.
5. Use a specific technical term when it is necessary for accuracy. Define it or link to its definition.
6. Keep noun groups short. Use prepositions to show relationships between terms.
7. Write procedures as direct instructions. State the condition, action, and expected result.
8. Use positive instructions when they are clear. State what the reader must do.
9. Use one spelling standard for the whole text. In English, use American spelling unless the user's style guide requires another variety. In Norwegian, see the "Norwegian" section.
10. Preserve code, commands, identifiers, product names, legal text, and required quotations. Do not simplify them silently.

When strict STE is not possible, keep the text clear and mark the terms or passages that need a domain-specific exception.

## Norwegian

Orwell's six rules and the STE writing rules above apply unchanged. These notes cover what Norwegian does differently. They agree with Språkrådet's klarspråk guidance; when a house style guide exists, the house style wins.

1. Choose bokmål or nynorsk and keep it for the whole text. Use the variety the user asks for; when the user does not say, use the variety of the source text, and default to bokmål for a new text. Do not mix the two.
2. Keep spelling forms consistent inside the chosen variety. Do not switch between "boka" and "boken", or between "-ene" and "-a" plurals, in the same text.
3. Write compounds as one word. Særskriving changes the meaning: "røykfritt område" is not "røyk fritt område". Check every noun group you shorten.
4. Cut substantivsjuke. Turn a nominalisation back into the verb it hides: "foreta en vurdering av" becomes "vurdere", "gi en beskrivelse av" becomes "beskrive", "ved innsending av" becomes "når du sender inn".
5. Prefer the active voice. Norwegian has both an s-passive ("saken behandles") and a bli-passive ("saken blir behandlet"); both hide the actor. Name the actor when the reader needs to know who acts: "vi behandler saken".
6. Address the reader as "du", and call the sender "vi". Avoid "man", "en", "vedkommende", and "søker" as stand-ins for the reader unless a legal or formal requirement calls for them.
7. Replace kanselli- and byråkratspråk with everyday words: "vedrørende" becomes "om", "i henhold til" becomes "etter" or "ifølge", "således" becomes "derfor", "å tilse" becomes "å sørge for". Use "i forhold til" only for a real comparison; otherwise use "om", "for", "sammenlignet med", or "når det gjelder".
8. Replace an anglicism when an established Norwegian word is just as precise: "deadline" becomes "frist", "issue" becomes "sak" or "feil". Keep the English term when it is the settled term in the field and a translation would be unclear.
9. Keep sentences short, but respect Norwegian word order. Do not clip a sentence into something ungrammatical to hit a word count.
10. Preserve code, commands, identifiers, product names, and quoted legal text exactly, including quotations from lov, forskrift, and avtaler. Do not rewrite a quoted statutory phrase into plainer Norwegian; explain it in a sentence next to the quote instead.

## Workflow

When writing from scratch:

1. Identify the audience, purpose, promised tone, and language from the user's request.
2. Draft in concrete, direct language.
3. Remove stock phrases, dead metaphors, filler, pompous diction, needless abstraction, and avoidable jargon.
4. Prefer active verbs and clear subjects unless passive voice better serves emphasis, tact, suspense, or technical accuracy.
5. Keep necessary nuance; do not make prose crude, false, or flat just to make it short.
6. Apply the ASD-STE100 baseline. Check terms, sentence structure, instructions, and technical exceptions. For Norwegian, apply the "Norwegian" section as well.

When revising existing text:

1. Preserve the user's meaning and any explicit tone or format constraints.
2. Cut words, clauses, and sentences that do no work.
3. Replace stale figures of speech with plain phrasing or a fresh, specific image.
4. Replace long, foreign, scientific, or jargon terms with everyday words when accuracy permits.
5. Convert passive constructions to active ones when the actor matters and is known.
6. Flag any remaining jargon, passive voice, or ornate phrasing that is necessary rather than silently removing important precision.
7. Run a final STE pass. Check that each technical term is consistent, each instruction states the required action, and each exception is intentional.
8. For Norwegian, run a final klarspråk pass. Check the variety and spelling forms, compounds, nominalisations, passives, and reader address.
